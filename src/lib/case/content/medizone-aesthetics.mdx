<Section id="overview" title="Overview">
	<Lead>

<Section id="problem" title="The problem">
	<P>
		The brand had a website, but it was an old WordPress build left unmaintained: out of date, and
		visibly behind the quality of the products. For buyers who are clinics, doctors and
		distributors, a neglected site reads as a neglected business.
	</P>
	<P>
		The rebuild started with an R&D pass on the B2B aesthetics space: how these products are
		evaluated, and by whom. The answer set the brief. The people searching arrive with technical
		questions. Which thread type for which procedure, which filler variant for which area, what
		training exists before they commit. If those answers are hard to find, the buyer moves on.
	</P>
	<P>
		So the site has two jobs at once: read as professional as the products, and answer clinical
		questions in clinical language without making the visitor dig.
	</P>
</Section>

<Section id="role" title="Role and constraints">
	<P>
		I ran the R&D, repositioned the site as a B2B catalog, and designed and built it end to end,
		including the CMS behind it.
	</P>
	<P>The constraints shaped everything:</P>
	<ul>
		<li>
			The reader is an expert. A dermatologist evaluating a thread range cannot be sold to with
			lifestyle copy; the site has to carry real specifications.
		</li>
		<li>
			The catalog changes weekly. New products, new training batches, new posts, and none of it can
			wait on a developer.
		</li>
		<li>
			Claims need care. These are clinical products, so every credential is presented as the brand
			presents it, and the design lets evidence carry the persuasion.
		</li>
	</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="decisions" title="Key decisions">
	<Subhead>Organize by clinical purpose, not by SKU</Subhead>
	<P>
		Practitioners think in procedures and use cases, so the catalog is grouped the way they think:
		threads for tissue lifting and collagen stimulation, fillers mapped to treatment areas,
		nutraceuticals as their own family. Each line gets its own space instead of one undifferentiated
		grid.
	</P>
	<Subhead>Put the numbers on the page</Subhead>
	<P>
		Professional buyers look for specifications, so the copy works at spec level: the Brili MDS
		strips list their 250 mg of L-glutathione and 20 mg of vitamin C per strip, the collagen shot
		its 7.5 g of hydrolyzed collagen. The detail is the persuasion.
	</P>
	<Subhead>Treat education as a product</Subhead>
	<P>
		For this audience, the support around the product is part of the product. Training is a
		first-class section with procedure videos and technical guidance, not a footer link, and the
		enquiry paths route by product interest so a conversation starts with context.
	</P>
</Section>

<Section id="trust" title="Trust as a feature">
	<P>
		For medical buyers, trust signals are content. The site foregrounds its product credentials,
		presenting the range as FDA-approved, and backs the claims with a before-and-after gallery so
		practitioners can judge documented results for themselves.
	</P>
	<P>
		The design choice is restraint: no lifestyle gloss, no urgency tricks. Evidence is given room,
		and the reader's expertise is treated as a given.
	</P>
</Section>
