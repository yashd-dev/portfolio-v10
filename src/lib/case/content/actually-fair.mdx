<Section id="problem" title="The problem">
	<P>
		Online stores often keep pricing and sourcing opaque. Reference prices are inflated, the sale
		never ends, and the shopper has no way to tell what anything should actually cost.
	</P>
	<P>
		Actually Fair's bet is that a store can win on the opposite: show fair pricing plainly, on every
		product, and let that be the brand. The catch is that this turns pricing into a design problem.
		If the math is legible on one page and hidden on the next, the promise collapses.
	</P>
	<P>
		There was a craft problem alongside the business one. The store ran on a stock Shopify theme,
		and the theme could no longer give the design the control the brand needed.
	</P>
	<Callout title="The real constraint">
		"Fair" only works if every surface shows the same math: the category grid, the product page, the
		cart and the checkout. One vague price anywhere and the whole brand reads as marketing.
	</Callout>
</Section>

<Section id="decisions" title="Key decisions">
	<Subhead>Headless over a theme</Subhead>
	<P>
		The store moved off the stock Shopify theme to a headless Next.js and Tailwind build. Shopify
		still owns products, cart and checkout; everything the shopper sees is ours to design. The
		tradeoff is real, a theme is free and headless is work, but the brand lives in the details of
		how prices and products are presented, and those details were exactly what the theme would not
		give up.
	</P>
	<Subhead>Transparency as a component rule</Subhead>
	<P>
		Fair pricing is enforced at the component level, not the copy level. Every product card shows
		the selling price next to the reference price with the percentage off, and the product page and
		cart repeat the same math in the same shape. It is a rule about what components must render,
		which is the only way a promise survives every screen.
	</P>
	<Subhead>Own the services where Shopify stops</Subhead>
	<P>
		Whatever Shopify does not cover became a small service we own, self-hosted on Railway: a reviews
		service so social proof lives in the store's own database, an order-ingestion webhook service
		that keeps internal tooling in sync as orders land, and a custom CMS, because Sanity was not a
		fit for this team's workload. Owning the services changes what features cost: something like
		Make A Wish, where shoppers ask for a product the catalog does not carry yet, is a small build
		inside our own stack instead of a plugin hunt. It turns a gap in inventory into a conversation,
		and every request is a signal about what to stock next.
	</P>
</Section>
